# Design Exploration 08 — Algorithms Worth Stealing

Scouting, not planning. Each entry is a real technique from somewhere, what it
would concretely change *here*, and what it would cost.

Ordered by what I'd actually reach for first, not by category.

---

## The two proven ones

Both are standard in procedural architecture. Neither is common in *sim games*,
which mostly use hand-authored building sets.

### 1. Split grammars (CGA Shape)

**Where from:** Müller, Wonka, Haegler, Ulmer & Van Gool, *Procedural Modeling of
Buildings*, SIGGRAPH 2006 — the engine behind Esri CityEngine, and the reason the
cities in Watch Dogs and the Assassin's Creed games have that density.

**What it is:** a building is not a model, it's a **derivation**. Start with a
mass. Apply rules that split it: mass → storeys → bays → panels → a window, a
door, a jetty, a beam. Each rule can branch on size, on which face it's on, or on
a random draw, so one grammar produces a whole street of related-but-different
buildings.

```
Building   → Storeys(groundFloor, upperFloors)
GroundFloor→ Bays(shopfront | door | window)
UpperFloor → Jetty(0.4) { Bays(window) }
```

**What it changes here:** `appearance.ts` is currently a hardcoded table of
constants per building type — eaves height, roof form, a dormer count. That
doesn't scale past about twenty buildings and every terrace is the same terrace.

A grammar makes **architecture a property of character** rather than of type. The
Devout grammar uses stone, tall narrow openings, buttresses. The Industrious
grammar uses regular bays, big windows, shallow pitch. Then a cottage *in* a
devout quarter is generated by the devout grammar, and the quarter looks coherent
because its buildings are literally built by the same rules. That's `04`'s
coherence expressed in the architecture itself instead of alongside it.

**Cost:** medium.

### ✅ Built — verdict: keep

Implemented as a **facade** grammar rather than a full mass grammar (`grammar.ts`):
it splits faces, not volumes, with massing still handled by `appearance.ts`. That
is where the visible variety lives and it was a fraction of the work.

A wall derives as face → storeys → bays → terminal, with a vocabulary per
character. Terminals so far: window, tall window, mullioned (leaded grid),
arched, shopfront, door, vent, loft, blank. Storey count and bay count come from
the wall's real dimensions, so a long warehouse gets many bays and a cottage two.

The rule sets are the point, because they are what makes a quarter legible:

| Character | Bay | Ground floor | Above | Blank wall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Industrious | 3.0m | windows, vents | windows | 10% |
| Mercantile | 3.4m | shopfronts | mullioned | 6% |
| Devout | 4.2m | arched | tall, narrow | **34%** |
| Rustic | 3.8m | small windows | small windows | 26% |

Devout leaving a third of its wall blank is doing as much work as any of the
openings — plain stone is what a church quarter mostly *is*.

**Two things it bought immediately.** Adding a building type is now a line rather
than a new block of constants, so the catalogue can grow past twenty without the
appearance table collapsing. And a blank-wall percentage turns out to convey
character better than any window shape does.

**Cost paid:** render CPU went from ~2.5ms to ~3.7–4.9ms per frame, since a
facade is now a dozen panels rather than a grid of identical rectangles. Worth
it, and there is obvious headroom (panels could be skipped below a zoom
threshold, which is the standard answer).

### ✅ Built, second half — the mass grammar (`massing.ts`)

The half deferred above is now built, and deferring it turned out to have been
the wrong call. "That is where the visible variety lives" was true of *facades*
and false of *buildings*: a street of boxes with well-derived walls is still a
street of boxes. The single biggest thing holding the look back was that every
building in the game was one rectangular prism with one roof on it, with its
proportions read from a table.

A building is now **derived into volumes** from a vocabulary of nine:

> main range · cross-wing · lean-to · jetty · porch · chancel · turret ·
> stack · veranda · plinth

and composed by the same axis the facade grammar uses:

> **Type says what a building is. Character says what it is built like. The
> grammar composes it out of volumes.**

So a merchant's house jetties its upper storey out over the street, a farmstead
grows a lean-to along its long wall and a byre at right angles, a chapel gets a
lower chancel, industry gets an annexe and a flue, and a keep is crenellated with
a corner turret — from eight rules, none of them written per building type.
Adding a type still costs a catalogue entry and nothing in the grammar.

**A roof form had to be added:** `lean`, a mono-pitch. It is what a lean-to, an
outshut and a veranda canopy all are, and it is the form that makes a building
look *added to over time* rather than built in one go. Nothing else in the
vocabulary does that.

**It lives in `render`, not `sim`,** and that is deliberate. Massing is
appearance: footprints, collision and placement all still use the catalogue's
flat width × depth, so a jetty overhangs visually and owns no ground. The
layering rule holds and the simulation stays engine-agnostic.

**Cost paid:** render CPU 3.73ms → 4.36ms on a matched 96-building scene,
measured against the previous commit built from a worktree. About 17% for a
large multiple of the geometry, which is the right trade.

### The lighting change that mattered as much as the grammar

Worth recording separately, because it was cheaper than any algorithm here and
did more for the picture than most of them:

> **A sunlit face goes warm. A shaded face does not go dark, it goes *blue* —
> because the only light reaching it is the sky.**

Every face in the game used to be shaded by mixing toward white or black. That is
greyscale shading, and it drifts the whole scene toward grey at both ends. Faces
now mix toward a warm sun colour or a cool sky colour instead, lit by their true
3D normal — key light plus hemispherical fill, for two lerps and no extra draw
calls. Terrain goes through the same function, so a hillside turning away from
the sun and a wall turning away from it agree, and the town sits *in* the
landscape rather than on top of it.

Two tuning findings, both the opposite of the obvious:

1. **Contrast must come from the shadow side, not the lit side.** A strong key
   washed every pale roof to cream — thatch already at `0xc0a765` mixed a third
   of the way toward a light warm colour has nowhere left to go. The key is now
   light (0.15) and the fill heavy (0.42), which is also how it works outdoors.
2. **Terrain relief must be measured against level ground, not against zero.**
   Feeding the raw lambert in tinted the entire map warm, because flat ground
   faces a sun 38° up and therefore reads as strongly lit — true, and visually
   useless, since it leaves no headroom for an actual hillside.

### 2. Straight skeleton

**Where from:** Aichholzer & Aurenhammer, 1995. The canonical application is
literally called the *roof model*.

**What it is:** shrink a polygon inward at constant speed from every edge; the
paths traced by the vertices are the skeleton. Raise the skeleton by the shrink
distance and you have a **correct hipped roof over any footprint** — including
the valleys and hips of an L-shape or a courtyard.

**What it changes here:** buildings are currently rectangles because my roof code
only handles rectangles. That's a real constraint on how a town can look — no
L-shaped farmhouse, no courtyard inn, no ranges round a churchyard.

Second use, arguably the better one: **plot subdivision**. Take a block bounded by
streets, offset inward, and split the frontage into plots. Which leads directly
to the next thing worth having —

**Burgage plots.** Medieval English towns divided street frontage into long
narrow strips: a few metres of frontage, a very long tail running back to a rear
lane. It's *why* medieval towns look the way they do, and why the back-lands have
that characteristic grain. My plots are naive rectangles hung behind each
building. Real burgage subdivision would give the town an authentically English
texture almost for free, and it would make the back lanes appear where they
actually belong.

**Cost:** the skeleton is genuinely fiddly to implement robustly (degenerate
cases, coincident events). Plot subdivision by recursive splitting gets 80% of the
look for 20% of the effort, so start there and only reach for the real skeleton if
the roofs demand it.

## The surprises

### 3. Anisotropic diffusion for the character field

**My favourite thing in this document.**

**Where from:** Perona & Malik, *Scale-space and edge detection using anisotropic
diffusion*, 1990. Image processing — blur an image without blurring across its
edges.

**What it is:** ordinary diffusion spreads equally in all directions. Anisotropic
diffusion is guided by a tensor field, so it spreads **fast along** some direction
and **slowly across** it, and can be blocked entirely at a boundary.

**What it changes here:** `field.ts` currently diffuses character with a symmetric
blur. That's a distance calculation wearing a costume — character spreads in
circles regardless of what's in the way.

Real character doesn't behave like that. It **runs along streets** and **stops at
walls, ridges and rivers**. Guide the diffusion by the fabric and the terrain, and:

- The tannery's grime follows the valley and the prevailing wind, exactly as `03`
  §2 wants, instead of forming a neat disc.
- A quarter behind a hill genuinely stays a separate place, even at 80m.
- Character bleeds *down the high street* and pools at the market — so the road
  network shapes the districts, tying `05` to `04` mechanically rather than
  thematically.
- Coherence becomes a **spatial puzzle** — "is there a ridge between my foundry
  and my church?" — rather than an arithmetic one about radii.

That last point matters most. It converts the character system from something you
reason about with distances into something you reason about with *terrain*, which
is the whole "spatial decisions in, arithmetic out" thesis.

**Cost:** low.

### ✅ Built — verdict: keep, clearly

Implemented as **geodesic emission** rather than iterated anisotropic diffusion,
which turned out to be the right call. Iterating a diffusion far enough to spread
100m would have taken hundreds of passes; instead each emission floods outward
cheapest-first through a cost field (`conductance.ts`) and stamps its falloff
against *accumulated cost* rather than straight-line distance. Same effect,
one Dijkstra per emission, fast enough to run on every edit.

Cost field: open ground 1, roads 0.4, worn paths 0.68, water 9, plus a
Perona–Malik edge-stopping term on slope so a scarp is nearly opaque and gentle
ground is transparent.

**Measured against the old behaviour** (flat cost makes geodesic ≡ Euclidean, so
it's a clean A/B, kept as the *Flow* toggle):

| | Town coherence | Cells covered |
|---|---|---|
| Geodesic (flow on) | **88%** | 7,933 |
| Euclidean (flow off) | 72% | 7,246 |

**I expected this to cost coherence and it gained 16 points.** The reasoning I had
was wrong: I assumed character bleeding along roads would mix the quarters
together. The opposite happens — overlapping *discs* were the thing muddling the
open ground between quarters, and channelling each quarter's character along its
own streets makes them interpenetrate **less**. Districts came out more distinct,
not less, and they cover more ground because road corridors carry them further.

Visually the fields are no longer discs: character streams down the high street,
pools where streets meet, and stops dead at the river.

Also added `field.townCoherence()` and a readout, because that A/B is the only
way to tell whether a change to the field helped, and eyeballing a coloured haze
is not.

### 4. Flow accumulation — rivers the terrain explains

**Where from:** D8 flow direction (O'Callaghan & Mark, 1984) plus priority-flood
depression filling (Barnes, Lehman & Mulla, 2014). Standard hydrology, used in
every GIS package.

**What it is:** for each cell, find its steepest downhill neighbour. Sort cells by
height, accumulate upstream area downhill in one pass. Cells with high
accumulation are where water collects. O(n log n), embarrassingly simple.

**What it changes here:** my river is a noise-warped channel *carved into* the
terrain — painted on, not derived. Flow accumulation instead says **where water
actually goes**, which gives:

- Streams that merge into rivers, with proper tributaries and a real catchment.
- Valleys that read as valleys because the water is in the bottom of them.
- **Ford and bridge sites** that are meaningfully better or worse.
- Floodplains, water meadows, and a reason the church is on the high ground.

And a genuinely nice gameplay hook: **a watermill needs flow and fall.** Multiply
accumulation by gradient and you have a map of the handful of places a mill can
go. That's a real spatial constraint, derived from the landscape rather than
invented as a rule — exactly the kind of decision `03` §1 argues for. The player
learns to read the river.

Pairs naturally with **hydraulic erosion** (droplet simulation) if we ever want
the terrain to look weathered rather than merely lumpy.

**Cost:** low.

### ✅ Built — verdict: keep, and it pulled a whole terrain toolset in with it

Done as a **terrain toolset** rather than a lone algorithm, which was the right
call — flow accumulation on a fixed heightmap is a fact about the map; flow
accumulation on a heightmap you can *reshape* is a toy.

Three parts:

- **`erosion.ts`** — droplet hydraulic erosion at generation. Fractal noise makes
  lumps, not landscape; it has no idea what water does. Erosion carves valleys
  that join up and lays sediment where the ground flattens.
- **`hydrology.ts`** — priority-flood ⇒ D8 ⇒ accumulation. Lakes are wherever the
  fill raised the ground; rivers are wherever enough catchment converges.
- **Terrain brushes** — raise, lower, level, at feature scale with a smooth
  falloff. **Every stroke re-derives the hydrology**, so damming a valley makes a
  lake because the fill pass now finds a depression, not because anything
  special-cases dams.

The old hand-carved river is gone. The map now has a **dendritic drainage
network** — tributaries branching out of the uplands, merging as they fall, and
reaching the sea — and the town sits on a coast rather than beside a painted
channel.

**Three bugs, all instructive:**

1. **No rivers at all, only ponds.** Priority-flood fills a depression to exactly
   level, and D8 cannot drain a **flat** — every neighbour is the same height, so
   the search finds no downhill and flow stops dead. Fixed with the standard
   Priority-Flood **+ ε** variant: each filled cell sits an epsilon above the one
   that reached it, so filled ground still drains.
2. **Parallel flow, no channels.** A tilted plane with fine noise drains in
   parallel — every cell to its own neighbour, nothing ever converging, so
   accumulation stays near zero everywhere. Drainage needs somewhere to
   *collect*, so the terrain was rebuilt with **low-frequency basins dominant**
   and fine noise only roughening them.
3. **Rivers rendering as dashed lines.** Not hydrology at all — the ground is
   drawn decimated at 8m blocks and I was sampling water the same way, so half of
   every one-cell-wide channel was skipped. Water is now drawn at full grid
   resolution, and widened by catchment so a trunk river reads as a river.

The third one is worth remembering as a class of mistake: I spent two rounds
tuning a simulation that was already correct, because the *view* of it was lying.

**Free gameplay hook, as hoped.** `millPotential` = √catchment × fall, readable in
the cursor readout as "good / possible / —". A watermill needs flow *and* drop,
so the handful of places one can go are a property of the landscape rather than a
rule the player has to be told.

### 5. Space colonisation — trees that are trees

**Where from:** Runions, Lane & Prusinkiewicz, *Modeling Trees with a Space
Colonization Algorithm*, 2007.

**What it is:** scatter attraction points in a volume shaped like a crown. Grow
branches from the trunk toward nearby attractors; each attractor is consumed once
reached. Branches compete for space and the result is a plausible, varied,
species-specific tree — without a single L-system rule.

**What it changes here:** my trees are two circles on a stick. They read fine at
distance and badly close up, and close up is where the charm has to survive.

Bake a dozen trees per species at load, render as instanced geometry, and get oaks
that fork low and spread, elms that go up, hedgerow trees that lean away from the
prevailing wind. The same algorithm also generates **hedge growth**, **ivy**, and —
because it's really a branching-network algorithm — **river networks** and **crack
patterns**, if we ever want them.

**Cost:** medium, but bounded: it's an offline bake, so performance is irrelevant.
The risk is that fully modelled trees may look *too* detailed beside deliberately
simple buildings — worth testing before committing.

### 6. Reaction–diffusion for the cultural border

**Where from:** Gray–Scott reaction–diffusion; Turing's 1952 morphogenesis paper is
the ancestor. It's how animal coat patterns are usually explained.

**What it is:** two substances diffusing at different rates while reacting with
each other. Depending on the parameters you get spots, stripes, travelling fronts,
fingering, or stable coexistence — from two lines of arithmetic per cell.

**What it changes here:** this is aimed squarely at the territory system in `01`,
which is the next thing to build.

The current design says pressure fields balance and the border sits where they
meet — a smooth contour. Which is *fine*, and slightly dull. Model the two
cultures as reacting substances instead and you get borders that:

- **Finger into each other** along a contested frontier rather than meeting flat.
- Form **enclaves** — a pocket of their culture stranded inside yours, which is
  historically the norm rather than the exception.
- Show **stable coexistence stripes** in genuinely mixed ground.
- Move in **travelling fronts** when one side gains, so change sweeps rather than
  creeping uniformly.

All of which makes the map worth *looking* at, which is the entire no-numbers
thesis. And it's honest: two competing populations diffusing and interacting is
literally what reaction–diffusion models.

**Cost:** low to implement, **high to tune.** Gray–Scott is notoriously
parameter-sensitive — most of the space is boring and a narrow band is
spectacular. Budget the time in tuning, not in coding.

### 7. Markov chains for names — the highest charm per line of code

**Where from:** ordinary character-level n-gram models, trained on a list of real
British place names.

**What it is:** learn which letter triples follow which, then sample. Feed it
Yorkshire and it gives you plausible Yorkshire. Feed it Cornwall and it gives you
Cornwall. A few hundred training names is plenty.

**What it changes here:** nothing is named. Not the town, not the quarters, not
one street.

Beyond the town name, the good bit is that **street names can be derived from
character**, and the game already knows the character of every square metre:

- Industrious + tanning → **Tanner's Row**, **The Shambles**, **Skinnergate**
- Mercantile → **Cornmarket**, **Cheapside**, **Silver Street**
- Devout → **Priory Lane**, **Paternoster Row**, **Kirkgate**
- Rustic → **Sheep Street**, **Millpond Lane**
- Raucous → **Grape Lane** (which really did mean what you think)

British towns name their streets after what happened on them, which is *exactly*
what the character field encodes. So the names would be **true** — a street called
Tanner's Row because there is genuinely a tannery on it — and that makes them a
legibility feature and not just decoration. When the tannery goes and the quarter
turns genteel, the name staying put is the town remembering (`00`, Pillar E).

**Cost:** trivial.

### ✅ Built — verdict: keep, with one half much stronger than the other

**Street naming: excellent, keep unreservedly.** It is rule-driven from real
vocabularies rather than sampled, so it cannot produce nonsense, and it is tied
to the actual dominant character along each road. The seeded test town came out
with **Cheapside, Cornmarket and Mercer Row** through the trading quarter and
**Skinnergate** through the working one. The names are *true*, which was the whole
argument for doing it.

Names are assigned once and never revised, so a street keeps its name after the
tannery that earned it has gone — Pillar E for free.

**Town naming: good but not flawless.** A 3rd-order character model over ~160 real
English place names. Sixteen samples:

> Padstock · Rington · Grimsthorne · Greenhoe · Helmscote Magna · Bridgeford ·
> Thwold · Ivybride · Oakham · Longstead · Thirlme · Holmere · Kings Kettleigh ·
> Ringham · Skelmdon · Penham

About twelve of sixteen are convincing. The failures are all the same failure:
the sampler stops mid-element and produces a truncated stub. A first pass was
much worse (*Thwell*, *Hensing*, *Stogumbe*); requiring the name to end in a
terminal cluster the corpus actually uses fixed most of it.

The survivors — *Thirlme*, *Ivybride* — pass because their endings genuinely do
occur (Den**holme**, Kirkbr**ide**), so a 3-character ending check can't catch
them. Fixable with a proper suffix list rather than a character window, but a
town name is drawn once per game, so an occasional odd one is cheap. Left as is.

## Two cheap wins worth noting

### ✅ Built — shading, shadows and a performance pass

**Sun and cast shadows.** One sun, low in the north-west, used consistently for
terrain relief, wall shading and cast shadows. A building's shadow is the convex
hull of its footprint swept along the light; trees get an offset blob; everything
gets a tight contact smudge where it meets the ground. Shadows are drawn flat in
a single layer under all upright geometry, so they need no depth sorting — they
simply darken whatever they fall across. Terrain relief switched from a fixed
cheap gradient to Lambert shading against the same sun, which is most of what
makes the landform read as landform.

### On measuring performance here — read this before trusting a number

This container renders with **SwiftShader**: software rasterisation, no GPU. Frame
rate here measures the absence of a graphics card, not the code. Every fps figure
below is therefore meaningless for real hardware and is recorded only to stop
anyone chasing it.

What *is* meaningful is **CPU time per frame**, which is ours, and generation
time. Both are now in the readout.

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Render CPU per frame | 7.58 ms | **~2.5 ms** |
| World generation | ~4.0 s | **~2.35 s** |
| Frame rate (software raster) | 2–4 | 2–4 — unchanged, and expected |

Roughly 98% of a frame here is software rasterisation. On a GPU this scene is
not remotely demanding.

**Where the CPU went, and one thing I should have caught sooner:**

- **Sculpting re-derived the entire hydrology on every pointer move** — a full
  flood-fill plus accumulation over 65k cells, per mouse event, while dragging.
  The heightmap now updates immediately so the ground responds under the cursor,
  and the water settles a beat after the stroke.
- **Accumulation was sorting 65k cells with a comparator on every terrain edit.**
  Priority-flood already pops in ascending filled height, so recording that order
  and walking it backwards gives the topological sort for nothing.
- **Erosion allocated an object per sample** — some 2.5 million allocations per
  generate. Scratch variables instead.
- **The sea was thousands of individual quads.** Standing water is a flat sheet,
  so each row is emitted as one run rather than eighty cells.
- **All 64 people bands were cleared every frame**, each clear forcing a geometry
  rebuild, even with nobody in them. Only bands that had someone last frame are
  touched now; empty static bands are hidden outright.

**The honest part:** at the previous commit this was already running at 4fps and
7.6ms of render CPU. None of it was caused by the terrain work — I had simply
never measured, through many turns of adding geometry. Two of the five problems
above (the per-move hydrology, the comparator sort) were mine from the turn
before; the other three had been there for ages.

**One tuning trap worth recording.** A channel has to be drawn wide enough to stay
continuous across the grid diagonal, or a stream renders as a string of beads. But
that gives every watercourse a floor on its apparent width, which turns the stream
threshold into a *visual* decision as much as a hydrological one: set it low and
every trickle looks like a river and the map turns to lace. Raised until only
courses worth showing appear.

**8. Poisson-disk sampling** (Bridson, 2007) for scatter. ✅ **Built — keep.**
The woodland used a jittered grid, which still ties every tree to a lattice cell,
and at distance the rows showed. Blue noise has no structure at any scale.

Worth recording how it combines with density: rather than varying the sampling
radius, samples are generated at uniform spacing and then **rejected** by the
density field. That keeps the even local spacing that makes it look like a wood,
while still letting copses and clearings form. Simpler than variable-radius
Poisson and, for this, indistinguishable.

**9. Baked ambient occlusion.** Flat-shaded low-poly lives or dies on contact
shadows. Even a cheap approximation — darken the ground within a metre or two of
anything standing on it, darken where a wall meets a roof — adds a lot of apparent
depth for very little work. Probably the best visual-improvement-per-hour on this
list, and it needs no clever algorithm at all.

## What I'd do, in order

1. **Anisotropic diffusion** (#3) — cheapest large change, and it makes the
   character system spatial rather than arithmetical.
2. **Names** (#7) — trivial, enormous charm, and it makes the town legible in a
   way nothing else here does.
3. **Flow accumulation** (#4) — a better landscape *and* a real siting constraint
   for mills.
4. **Baked AO** (#9) and **Poisson disk** (#8) — small, visible, boring, worth it.
5. **Split grammars** (#1) — the big one for buildings, and the right moment is
   before the building catalogue grows past twenty types.
6. **Reaction–diffusion** (#6) — when territory gets built, not before.
7. **Space colonisation** (#5) — last, and only if close-up trees turn out to
   matter.
