# Design Exploration 08 — Algorithms Worth Stealing

Scouting, not planning. Each entry is a real technique from somewhere, what it
would concretely change *here*, and what it would cost.

Ordered by what I'd actually reach for first, not by category.

---

## The two proven ones

Both are standard in procedural architecture. Neither is common in *sim games*,
which mostly use hand-authored building sets.

### 1. Split grammars (CGA Shape)

**Where from:** Müller, Wonka, Haegler, Ulmer & Van Gool, *Procedural Modeling of
Buildings*, SIGGRAPH 2006 — the engine behind Esri CityEngine, and the reason the
cities in Watch Dogs and the Assassin's Creed games have that density.

**What it is:** a building is not a model, it's a **derivation**. Start with a
mass. Apply rules that split it: mass → storeys → bays → panels → a window, a
door, a jetty, a beam. Each rule can branch on size, on which face it's on, or on
a random draw, so one grammar produces a whole street of related-but-different
buildings.

```
Building   → Storeys(groundFloor, upperFloors)
GroundFloor→ Bays(shopfront | door | window)
UpperFloor → Jetty(0.4) { Bays(window) }
```

**What it changes here:** `appearance.ts` is currently a hardcoded table of
constants per building type — eaves height, roof form, a dormer count. That
doesn't scale past about twenty buildings and every terrace is the same terrace.

A grammar makes **architecture a property of character** rather than of type. The
Devout grammar uses stone, tall narrow openings, buttresses. The Industrious
grammar uses regular bays, big windows, shallow pitch. Then a cottage *in* a
devout quarter is generated by the devout grammar, and the quarter looks coherent
because its buildings are literally built by the same rules. That's `04`'s
coherence expressed in the architecture itself instead of alongside it.

**Cost:** medium. A small interpreter plus a rule set per character. The
interpreter is a weekend; the rule sets are where the real work lives, and they
never stop being tunable.

### 2. Straight skeleton

**Where from:** Aichholzer & Aurenhammer, 1995. The canonical application is
literally called the *roof model*.

**What it is:** shrink a polygon inward at constant speed from every edge; the
paths traced by the vertices are the skeleton. Raise the skeleton by the shrink
distance and you have a **correct hipped roof over any footprint** — including
the valleys and hips of an L-shape or a courtyard.

**What it changes here:** buildings are currently rectangles because my roof code
only handles rectangles. That's a real constraint on how a town can look — no
L-shaped farmhouse, no courtyard inn, no ranges round a churchyard.

Second use, arguably the better one: **plot subdivision**. Take a block bounded by
streets, offset inward, and split the frontage into plots. Which leads directly
to the next thing worth having —

**Burgage plots.** Medieval English towns divided street frontage into long
narrow strips: a few metres of frontage, a very long tail running back to a rear
lane. It's *why* medieval towns look the way they do, and why the back-lands have
that characteristic grain. My plots are naive rectangles hung behind each
building. Real burgage subdivision would give the town an authentically English
texture almost for free, and it would make the back lanes appear where they
actually belong.

**Cost:** the skeleton is genuinely fiddly to implement robustly (degenerate
cases, coincident events). Plot subdivision by recursive splitting gets 80% of the
look for 20% of the effort, so start there and only reach for the real skeleton if
the roofs demand it.

## The surprises

### 3. Anisotropic diffusion for the character field

**My favourite thing in this document.**

**Where from:** Perona & Malik, *Scale-space and edge detection using anisotropic
diffusion*, 1990. Image processing — blur an image without blurring across its
edges.

**What it is:** ordinary diffusion spreads equally in all directions. Anisotropic
diffusion is guided by a tensor field, so it spreads **fast along** some direction
and **slowly across** it, and can be blocked entirely at a boundary.

**What it changes here:** `field.ts` currently diffuses character with a symmetric
blur. That's a distance calculation wearing a costume — character spreads in
circles regardless of what's in the way.

Real character doesn't behave like that. It **runs along streets** and **stops at
walls, ridges and rivers**. Guide the diffusion by the fabric and the terrain, and:

- The tannery's grime follows the valley and the prevailing wind, exactly as `03`
  §2 wants, instead of forming a neat disc.
- A quarter behind a hill genuinely stays a separate place, even at 80m.
- Character bleeds *down the high street* and pools at the market — so the road
  network shapes the districts, tying `05` to `04` mechanically rather than
  thematically.
- Coherence becomes a **spatial puzzle** — "is there a ridge between my foundry
  and my church?" — rather than an arithmetic one about radii.

That last point matters most. It converts the character system from something you
reason about with distances into something you reason about with *terrain*, which
is the whole "spatial decisions in, arithmetic out" thesis.

**Cost:** low. It's the same blur loop with per-cell weights instead of fixed
ones. Genuinely a day's work for a large change in behaviour.

### 4. Flow accumulation — rivers the terrain explains

**Where from:** D8 flow direction (O'Callaghan & Mark, 1984) plus priority-flood
depression filling (Barnes, Lehman & Mulla, 2014). Standard hydrology, used in
every GIS package.

**What it is:** for each cell, find its steepest downhill neighbour. Sort cells by
height, accumulate upstream area downhill in one pass. Cells with high
accumulation are where water collects. O(n log n), embarrassingly simple.

**What it changes here:** my river is a noise-warped channel *carved into* the
terrain — painted on, not derived. Flow accumulation instead says **where water
actually goes**, which gives:

- Streams that merge into rivers, with proper tributaries and a real catchment.
- Valleys that read as valleys because the water is in the bottom of them.
- **Ford and bridge sites** that are meaningfully better or worse.
- Floodplains, water meadows, and a reason the church is on the high ground.

And a genuinely nice gameplay hook: **a watermill needs flow and fall.** Multiply
accumulation by gradient and you have a map of the handful of places a mill can
go. That's a real spatial constraint, derived from the landscape rather than
invented as a rule — exactly the kind of decision `03` §1 argues for. The player
learns to read the river.

Pairs naturally with **hydraulic erosion** (droplet simulation) if we ever want
the terrain to look weathered rather than merely lumpy.

**Cost:** low. A sort and two passes. The hard part is deciding thresholds.

### 5. Space colonisation — trees that are trees

**Where from:** Runions, Lane & Prusinkiewicz, *Modeling Trees with a Space
Colonization Algorithm*, 2007.

**What it is:** scatter attraction points in a volume shaped like a crown. Grow
branches from the trunk toward nearby attractors; each attractor is consumed once
reached. Branches compete for space and the result is a plausible, varied,
species-specific tree — without a single L-system rule.

**What it changes here:** my trees are two circles on a stick. They read fine at
distance and badly close up, and close up is where the charm has to survive.

Bake a dozen trees per species at load, render as instanced geometry, and get oaks
that fork low and spread, elms that go up, hedgerow trees that lean away from the
prevailing wind. The same algorithm also generates **hedge growth**, **ivy**, and —
because it's really a branching-network algorithm — **river networks** and **crack
patterns**, if we ever want them.

**Cost:** medium, but bounded: it's an offline bake, so performance is irrelevant.
The risk is that fully modelled trees may look *too* detailed beside deliberately
simple buildings — worth testing before committing.

### 6. Reaction–diffusion for the cultural border

**Where from:** Gray–Scott reaction–diffusion; Turing's 1952 morphogenesis paper is
the ancestor. It's how animal coat patterns are usually explained.

**What it is:** two substances diffusing at different rates while reacting with
each other. Depending on the parameters you get spots, stripes, travelling fronts,
fingering, or stable coexistence — from two lines of arithmetic per cell.

**What it changes here:** this is aimed squarely at the territory system in `01`,
which is the next thing to build.

The current design says pressure fields balance and the border sits where they
meet — a smooth contour. Which is *fine*, and slightly dull. Model the two
cultures as reacting substances instead and you get borders that:

- **Finger into each other** along a contested frontier rather than meeting flat.
- Form **enclaves** — a pocket of their culture stranded inside yours, which is
  historically the norm rather than the exception.
- Show **stable coexistence stripes** in genuinely mixed ground.
- Move in **travelling fronts** when one side gains, so change sweeps rather than
  creeping uniformly.

All of which makes the map worth *looking* at, which is the entire no-numbers
thesis. And it's honest: two competing populations diffusing and interacting is
literally what reaction–diffusion models.

**Cost:** low to implement, **high to tune.** Gray–Scott is notoriously
parameter-sensitive — most of the space is boring and a narrow band is
spectacular. Budget the time in tuning, not in coding.

### 7. Markov chains for names — the highest charm per line of code

**Where from:** ordinary character-level n-gram models, trained on a list of real
British place names.

**What it is:** learn which letter triples follow which, then sample. Feed it
Yorkshire and it gives you plausible Yorkshire. Feed it Cornwall and it gives you
Cornwall. A few hundred training names is plenty.

**What it changes here:** nothing is named. Not the town, not the quarters, not
one street.

Beyond the town name, the good bit is that **street names can be derived from
character**, and the game already knows the character of every square metre:

- Industrious + tanning → **Tanner's Row**, **The Shambles**, **Skinnergate**
- Mercantile → **Cornmarket**, **Cheapside**, **Silver Street**
- Devout → **Priory Lane**, **Paternoster Row**, **Kirkgate**
- Rustic → **Sheep Street**, **Millpond Lane**
- Raucous → **Grape Lane** (which really did mean what you think)

British towns name their streets after what happened on them, which is *exactly*
what the character field encodes. So the names would be **true** — a street called
Tanner's Row because there is genuinely a tannery on it — and that makes them a
legibility feature and not just decoration. When the tannery goes and the quarter
turns genteel, the name staying put is the town remembering (`00`, Pillar E).

**Cost:** trivial. Half a day, and it's probably the single largest increase in
charm available per line of code in this whole document.

## Two cheap wins worth noting

**8. Poisson-disk sampling** (Bridson, 2007) for scatter. My woodland uses a
jittered grid, which leaves faint rows visible at distance. Bridson's algorithm is
O(n), about thirty lines, and gives natural spacing with no structure. Small,
strictly better.

**9. Baked ambient occlusion.** Flat-shaded low-poly lives or dies on contact
shadows. Even a cheap approximation — darken the ground within a metre or two of
anything standing on it, darken where a wall meets a roof — adds a lot of apparent
depth for very little work. Probably the best visual-improvement-per-hour on this
list, and it needs no clever algorithm at all.

---

## What I'd do, in order

1. **Anisotropic diffusion** (#3) — cheapest large change, and it makes the
   character system spatial rather than arithmetical.
2. **Names** (#7) — trivial, enormous charm, and it makes the town legible in a
   way nothing else here does.
3. **Flow accumulation** (#4) — a better landscape *and* a real siting constraint
   for mills.
4. **Baked AO** (#9) and **Poisson disk** (#8) — small, visible, boring, worth it.
5. **Split grammars** (#1) — the big one for buildings, and the right moment is
   before the building catalogue grows past twenty types.
6. **Reaction–diffusion** (#6) — when territory gets built, not before.
7. **Space colonisation** (#5) — last, and only if close-up trees turn out to
   matter.
