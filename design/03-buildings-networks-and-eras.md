# Design Exploration 04 — Buildings, Networks and Eras (Game B)

The city builder. Medieval start, progressing through to the SimCity register.

This document answers one question first, because it turns out to govern
everything else: **what is the player's brush, and what is beneath it?**

---

## 1. The resolution line

The instinct behind "parks and lakes yes, individual trees no / rails and stations
yes, bus timetables no / power and water lines yes, not too finicky" is one rule,
and it's worth stating precisely because it settles arguments before they start.

> **The player's brush is the smallest thing that changes the simulation.
> Everything below that resolution is generated by the world, not placed by you.**

Two tests. A candidate mechanic has to pass **both**:

**Test 1 — Is the decision spatial?**
*Where* is a decision. *How much*, *how often*, and *in what order* are admin.
Lay a rail line: spatial, keep it. Set its timetable: operational, cut it.

**Test 2 — Does the placement have a consequence?**
If two different placements produce the same outcome, it isn't a decision, it's
labour. A park changes its neighbourhood. A single tree changes nothing.

This explains every instinct in one go:

| | Spatial? | Consequential? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Park, lake, wood | Yes | Yes — changes the area | **Place it** |
| Individual tree | Yes | No | **Generated** |
| Rail line, station | Yes | Yes — huge | **Place it** |
| Bus timetable | No | Yes | **Cut it** |
| Water/power line | Yes | Yes — reach | **Place it** |
| Capacity balancing | No | Yes | **Cut it** |

### "No individual trees" doesn't mean no trees

It means trees are **not your job**. They come with parks, they line roads, they
grow in gardens, they fill the gaps. You get the density and the charm without the
labour.

This is already in the design — it's Pillar B's infill from `00`. The player
supplies **structure**; evolution supplies **detail**. Trees, hedges, benches,
fences, walls, washing lines, market stalls: all generated, never placed.

**The slogan for the network case: you draw, you never dispatch.**

## 2. The vibe system — and the moment the whole design connects

> ⚠️ **Superseded by `04-character-and-districts.md`.** The pleasant/grim framing
> below was wrong — character is plural and qualitative, not a quality axis. The
> *chain* described here survives and is the important part; the scalar model of
> vibe does not. Read `04` for the current system.

"Things that affect the vibe of the area around them" is the most important thing
in your list, because it's the connective tissue the design was missing.

Every building emits **character** into its surroundings. ~~On a few readable axes:
pleasant/grim, quiet/loud, grand/humble.~~ Corrected in `04`: a location carries a
*blend* of many qualitative characters, and what's read off it is the **dominant**
one plus **coherence** — not a score.

### The chain

Vibe isn't a separate stat. It's the **input to systems that already exist**, and
wiring it in completes a single causal chain that runs from your smallest action
to the largest one in the game:

> **Placement → Vibe → Evolution → Culture → Territory**

You place a park. The area becomes pleasant. Nearby houses flourish into townhouses
(`00`, Pillar B). Flourishing districts radiate cultural pressure (`01`). Your
border drifts outward.

And in reverse: you place a foundry. The area turns grim. Houses rot into slums.
Rotting districts radiate nothing. **Your border recedes.**

Which yields the sentence that makes this game make sense:

> **A park is a territorial act.**

Every building category now feeds one loop. Nothing is bolted on. This is the thing
that was missing.

### The tension it creates, for free

You **need** the foundry — it's your economy and your army. But it poisons what it
touches. So you learn to put industry downwind, over the hill, away from the good
streets.

**Zoning emerges as a player strategy without a zoning tool.** You separate your
districts because you can *see* the consequences of not doing it, not because the
game has a zoning layer. Districts acquire distinct character through your
decisions rather than through a painting UI. That's the SimCity outcome arrived at
the Settlers way.

### Wind and terrain (worth prototyping)

Let character **flow**: smoke drifts downwind, a hill blocks it, a river valley
channels it. One extra parameter, highly legible (you can *see* the smoke), and it
turns industrial siting into a proper spatial puzzle with real regional character.

Watch for over-engineering — but wind is one variable and it earns its place. It
matters more under `04`'s model, where flow determines which quarter a character
bleeds into and therefore where coherence breaks down.

## 3. The building families

Your four, plus two things that are really *tools* rather than families:

**Two tools** (different verbs, different feel):
- **Housing** — you place it yourself (`00`, Axis 1). Siting quality drives
  evolution, so this is where vibe is felt most directly.
- **Networks** — drawn, not placed. See §4.

**Four building families:**

| Family | Examples | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic** | Sawmill, smelter, farm, mill, workshop, market, dock, warehouse | The 3-node chains from `00`, Axis 3 |
| **Civic** | Church, town hall, school, tavern, baths, theatre, park, square | The widest range of character emitters; sets what a quarter *is* |
| **Military** | Tower, keep, barracks, wall, arsenal | Military pressure sources from `01` |
| **Utility** | Well, conduit, sewer, gasworks, waterworks, power station | Network sources. See §5 |

**Every family can win you territory**, because cultural pressure comes from
strength of character rather than from niceness (`04`). A dockside, a foundry
quarter and a genteel crescent all radiate — differently. What matters is that each
quarter is *coherently* something.

Civic is still the most *expressive* family: it has the widest range of characters
available to it, so it's the main tool for deciding what a district becomes rather
than merely what it produces.

## 4. Transport — the rollercoaster feeling

You're right that this is where the tactile building pleasure lives. RollerCoaster
Tycoon's track builder is one of the best construction interfaces ever made, and
the reasons are copyable: **piece-by-piece construction, real constraints to work
against, immediate visual feedback, and then you watch the thing you built do its
job.**

### Two tools, two feels — deliberately

**Roads are painted.** Quick, loose, forgiving. Drag, it snaps, done. This is your
sketching tool and it should never feel precious.

**Rails are engineered.** Piece by piece. Gradients matter. Curves have radii.
Hills mean a cutting, a tunnel, or a detour. Rivers mean a bridge or a viaduct.
Terrain genuinely fights you, and a well-built line is *visibly* well-built.

Keeping these as separate tools with separate feels means the casual pleasure and
the craft pleasure don't dilute each other.

**The craft is in the engineering, never in the operating.** You solve "how do I get
a line through that ridge to that town." You never touch frequency, capacity, or
scheduling. Trains just run, and you watch them.

### Stations are the interesting placement

A station is where the engineered network meets the painted town, and it's a
placement with big consequences: **development pulls toward it.** Put a station
somewhere and the town leans that way. That's a genuine strategic decision made
entirely spatially.

### Canals

Between cart tracks and railways sits the canal age, and it's perfect for this
treatment — locks, gradients, cuttings, aqueducts, a barge moving slowly through
country you shaped. It's the rollercoaster-engineering pleasure at its purest, it's
enormously characterful, and games almost never do it.

## 5. Utilities — reach, never capacity

Apply the resolution line. What's spatial and consequential about a utility?
**Reach.** Does it get there or not?

What's finicky? Pressure, load balancing, pump sizing, brownout cascades, capacity
arithmetic. All of it fails Test 1.

**The rules:**
- You lay lines. Connected = served. Not connected = not served. That's the model.
- Sources (well, reservoir, gasworks, power station) have a generous radius; lines
  extend it.
- **No capacity number to balance.** When a source is overstretched it **visibly
  strains** — the reservoir level drops, the power station smokes harder, the
  pressure in the fountain falls. You see it and you build another.
- No cascading failures. No blackout to diagnose. Nothing to *tune*, only things to
  *extend*.

## 6. Eras — each one is a new network, not just newer buildings

The weak version of era progression is "buildings get bigger and shinier." The
strong version, which fits everything above:

> **Each era hands you a new network layer and a new spatial puzzle.**

| Era | New networks | New character |
|---|---|---|
| **Medieval** | Cart tracks, wells, middens | Walls matter. Water is a walk away. |
| **Early modern** | Canals, conduits, public fountains | Trade reaches inland. Squares and civic pride. |
| **Victorian** | **Railways, sewers, gas lighting** | The transformative era. Sanitation redeems slums; gaslight makes streets pleasant at night. |
| **Modern** | Motor roads, electricity, telephone | Speed, sprawl, and the pressure to demolish. |

So eras feel different because **you're doing different engineering**, not because
the art changed. Sewers arriving is a genuine event — whole districts become
liveable that never could be before, which visibly moves vibe, evolution, culture
and territory in one cascade.

### How eras arrive

Not a tech tree to click through — a tech tree is a menu, and menus are admin.
**Eras are earned by the state of your town** (prosperity, population, a key
building) and **arrive as events**: *the railway is coming.* That fits real-time
with interruptions from `00`, Axis 6, and it makes progression a story rather than
a purchase.

### The hard rule: eras never obsolete your buildings

Nothing is auto-demolished. Nothing becomes unusable. New layers go **on top** of
old ones — the medieval cart track is still there under the Victorian high street,
and your first church is still standing in 1900, aged and patched and surrounded by
terraces.

This is Pillar E (`00`) and it's the entire emotional payoff of era progression.
The joy of medieval-to-SimCity is *seeing what survived*.

## 7. Terrain

Consistent with §1: terrain is edited at **feature scale, with a brush** — dig a
lake, raise a bank, plant a wood, cut a terrace. Never vertex-by-vertex sculpting.

A dug lake is a placement with a consequence (vibe, water, beauty). A single raised
vertex is labour.

## 8. Open questions

1. ~~How many vibe axes?~~ **Resolved in `04`:** not axes at all. A palette of
   10–14 qualitative characters, read as dominant-plus-coherence. Open questions
   moved to `04` §6.
2. **Does the road/rail split confuse?** Two transport tools with deliberately
   different feels is unusual. It could read as inconsistent rather than as range.
3. **Does wind over-complicate industrial siting**, or is it the best puzzle in the
   game? Genuinely unsure. Cheap to test.
4. **Era pacing.** Four eras across one session is a lot of content. Might be
   better as fewer, longer eras — or the campaign spans several sessions.
5. **Do utilities have enough game in them** once capacity is removed? Reach alone
   may be too thin by the modern era, when everything is trivially connected.
   Sewers and gas have strong vibe effects, which may carry it — electricity may
   not.
